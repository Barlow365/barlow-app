The Talent Pipeline Nobody Built

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
July 16, 2026 8 min read

There are roughly 600,000 open skilled trades positions in the United States right now, according to the Associated Builders and Contractors. Companies in Detroit, Cleveland, and Baltimore are posting the same jobs month after month, watching applications trickle in, and concluding there is a talent shortage. There is not a talent shortage. There is a pipeline shortage — and the difference matters enormously, because one implies the problem is with the people and the other puts it squarely on the system.

What We Call a Shortage Is Usually a Gap We Created

When manufacturers in southeast Michigan say they cannot find welders, machinists, or CNC operators, they are often describing a condition their own industry helped produce. Vocational programs were systematically defunded over two decades. Shop classes got cut. Community colleges shifted resources toward four-year transfer pathways because that is where state funding formulas pointed. The result was predictable: the supply of trained trades workers declined while demand from advanced manufacturing, construction, and logistics kept climbing.

This is not a Detroit-specific story, but Detroit makes it visible faster than most places. The city shed population, then industry restructured, then workforce systems were patched together from federal grants and philanthropic pilots. None of it added up to a durable pipeline. Meanwhile, the skilled workers who stayed often aged out without a replacement cohort behind them. We built the hole ourselves, then acted surprised when we fell in.

Cleveland has a version of the same story. Baltimore has its own. What they share is a decade-plus gap between when industry started signaling its needs and when any serious institutional response materialized. That gap does not close on its own. Someone has to build the infrastructure.

The Workforce System That Was Never Designed to Work

The federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act, WIOA, distributes roughly $2.8 billion annually to states for workforce development, according to the Department of Labor. That sounds significant until you understand how fragmented the delivery becomes at the local level. Funds flow through state agencies to local workforce boards, then to training providers, with each handoff adding reporting requirements and shrinking the actual dollars reaching trainees. A worker trying to get certified in industrial maintenance might touch four or five separate programs, each with different eligibility rules, different timelines, and different definitions of completion.

This architecture was not designed with the worker at the center. It was designed to satisfy multiple funders simultaneously. The result is a system that looks active from the outside — lots of programs, lots of organizations, lots of press releases — but produces inconsistent outcomes at scale. Employers in Detroit's Corktown and Midtown corridors will tell you they have been to workforce convenings for years without seeing the candidate volume they actually need. That is not a knock on the people running those meetings. That is a structural diagnosis.

The other design flaw is timeline. Most workforce programs are funded in one to three year cycles. Building a meaningful pipeline from high school students or career changers into certified industrial roles takes four to six years minimum if you are being honest about credential attainment, employer relationship-building, and retention. You cannot build long infrastructure on short money.

We know what works. We just have not built it at scale.

What Employers Got Wrong Too

Industry is not an innocent bystander here. For years, many employers maintained the posture that workforce development was someone else's job — the schools, the government, the training providers. Some companies wanted fully credentialed workers with three to five years of experience, starting at wages that did not reflect what it takes to live in a city with rising rents. Then they described the resulting empty applicant pool as evidence that workers did not want to work.

The companies that cracked this in Detroit — and some have — did it by getting uncomfortable with their own assumptions. They partnered directly with Detroit at Work, with community colleges, with high schools. They created earn-while-you-learn models. They looked at their own job descriptions and stripped out requirements that had nothing to do with actual job performance. When you require a four-year degree to run a forklift or manage a warehouse shift, you are not screening for competence. You are screening out the population you claim you cannot find.

Apprenticeship is the model that consistently works, and the data backs this up. The Department of Labor reports that apprentices earn an average of $77,000 annually after completing their programs, with a 94 percent job retention rate. Those are not marginal outcomes. That is a reliable return. But registered apprenticeship programs in the United States cover fewer than one percent of the workforce. We know what works. We just have not built it at scale.

The Infrastructure Bet Nobody Wants to Make

Building a real pipeline requires someone to hold the long position. That means a company, a city agency, a philanthropy, or some combination committing to a ten-year relationship with a school district, not a three-year pilot. It means funding coordinators who sit inside schools and understand both employer needs and student realities. It means employers agreeing to take candidates who are 80 percent ready and training the remaining 20 percent internally, rather than waiting for a fully formed worker to appear.

Detroit has some pieces of this. The Detroit at Work network has improved coordination. The Manufacturing Renaissance work in Chicago shows what a sustained institutional commitment can produce when a city decides to treat manufacturing as a civic asset rather than an economic relic. Neither is fully there yet, but both demonstrate that the gap is closeable when the right parties decide to hold position through the hard middle years when outcomes are not yet visible.

The missing piece in most cities is an entity that carries the full pipeline relationship — from eighth grade career exposure through first year of employment — without handing the worker off at every stage. Every handoff is a dropout risk. Every new intake form is a reason for someone working two jobs to walk away from a training opportunity. Whoever builds that continuous relationship, and funds it durably, is not doing charity work. They are building the most reliable source of skilled labor in their region.

This Is a Systems Build, Not a Program Launch

The language we use shapes what we are willing to fund. Programs are temporary by definition. Systems are meant to outlast their founders. Detroit's industrial recovery, such as it is, will not be sustained by a rotating set of workforce pilots. It will be sustained by embedded relationships between anchor employers, community colleges, and high schools that have been running long enough to trust each other and iterate on what is not working.

Other cities are making the same bet with different results depending almost entirely on whether someone in the room was willing to take the long view and stay. That is the real constraint. Not money, not candidate quality, not employer demand. Staying power. The willingness to build something that takes longer to prove than a grant cycle allows.

Next, we should look at where apprenticeship infrastructure has actually scaled in the U.S. and what made the difference in those cases — because the answer is not what most workforce development professionals expect.

The talent pipeline problem is solvable. It is not waiting on a new technology or a federal policy breakthrough. It is waiting on institutions — employers, cities, schools, funders — to stop optimizing for the short cycle and start building for the long one. Detroit has enough runway left to do this right. The question is whether the people with the resources to hold the long position will make that call before the next wave of employers decides the city is not worth the investment. That decision is not abstract. It is happening right now, in boardrooms and budget meetings, and most of those conversations do not include anyone who actually lives in the neighborhoods where the workers we say we cannot find are already waiting.
