The Org That Runs on Relationships Instead of Processes

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
September 29, 2026 7 min read

There is a nonprofit in Detroit that has served the same neighborhoods for over twenty years. It has done real work — connected families to housing, placed workers in trades jobs, moved the needle in measurable ways. Ask anyone inside how it works and you will get the same answer: 'We know everybody.' That answer is also the organization's greatest structural liability. According to a 2023 report from the Bridgespan Group, roughly 67 percent of nonprofits lack a documented succession plan, and leadership transitions are the single most common trigger for organizational collapse. The org that runs on relationships is not just fragile — it is one retirement party away from losing decades of institutional knowledge with no transfer mechanism in place.

What 'We Know Everybody' Actually Means

When someone says their organization runs on relationships, they usually mean a specific thing: key individuals hold access. A program director who calls the housing authority contact directly. An executive who can get a city council member on the phone. A long-tenured case manager who remembers which landlord will actually respond and which one won't. These are real assets. They took years to build. The problem is they live inside people, not inside systems.

That distinction matters more than most leaders want to admit. A relationship stored in someone's head is not an organizational asset — it is a personal one. When that person leaves, the relationship does not transfer. The new hire starts from zero. The referral pipeline dries up. The housing contact stops returning calls because she does not know who is on the other end anymore. The org does not lose a staff member; it loses a node in a network that was never mapped.

We have seen this play out in Detroit repeatedly, particularly in the workforce development sector where relationships with employers are everything. Organizations that placed hundreds of workers per year watched placement rates drop by half after a single director transition — not because the programs got worse, but because the connective tissue was human and unarchived.

The Founder Shadow Problem

This is most acute in founder-led organizations, where the founding personality is so central to operations that the org has essentially been built around one person's charisma and contact list. A McKinsey study on nonprofit leadership found that organizations led by their founders for more than a decade are significantly more likely to experience mission drift and governance breakdown during transition than those that professjonalized leadership structures earlier. The org becomes a reflection of a person rather than an expression of a system.

Detroit is not unique here. Cleveland has the same pattern in its community development sector. Philadelphia has it in arts organizations that launched in the 1980s and 1990s on founder energy and have never institutionalized what made them work. The founder is not the problem. The founder usually built something real. The problem is what was never built alongside it: the documentation, the process architecture, the relationship infrastructure that can survive a personnel change.

Operating multiple organizations simultaneously makes this dynamic visible in a way that running one org does not. When you watch the same failure mode appear across different sectors and geographies, you stop blaming the people and start examining the structure. The structure is the problem. The people are just working inside a system that was never designed to outlast them.

A relationship stored in someone's head is not an organizational asset — it is a personal one.

What Gets Lost and What It Costs

The cost of relationship-dependent organizations is not abstract. It shows up in service gaps, in grant funding that follows a charismatic leader to their next role, in community trust that erodes when the face of an organization changes and residents do not know what to expect. A 2021 survey by CompassPoint found that 50 percent of nonprofit executive directors planned to leave their positions within five years, and of those, the majority reported that their organizations were not prepared for the transition. That is not a leadership pipeline problem alone — it is a systems documentation problem.

It also shows up in vendor and partner relationships. When a city agency has a contact at an org and that contact leaves, the agency does not automatically redirect to the new contact. They pause. They reassess. Sometimes they redirect funding to a different organization entirely — one that feels more stable, more institutionalized, more like a system than a personality. Relationship-dependent orgs lose contracts this way without ever understanding why.

What a Process-Supported Relationship Actually Looks Like

This is not an argument against relationships. Relationships are load-bearing in community work. The argument is that relationships need scaffolding — documentation, handoff protocols, CRM systems that actually capture context and not just contact information, and organizational cultures that treat knowledge transfer as a core discipline rather than an afterthought during an exit interview.

A well-built organization does not replace the relationship — it replicates the conditions that made the relationship possible. That means a new program director knows not just who to call at the housing authority, but why that contact trusts the organization, what history they share, what commitments were made and kept, and what sensitivities exist in the relationship. That is transferable. It requires intentional documentation culture, but it is buildable.

In Detroit's manufacturing ecosystem, the best workforce intermediaries figured this out because they had to. Employer relationships in skilled trades require consistency across years and across staff transitions. The organizations that survived and scaled created account management structures, regular touchpoints that were calendared and assigned at the org level rather than the individual level, and contact histories that lived in shared systems. They treated employer relationships the way a good sales organization treats key accounts — with infrastructure, not just charisma.

The Audit Most Orgs Skip

Most organizations do not audit their relationship dependencies because the question feels uncomfortable. Mapping which relationships are held by individuals versus the organization as a whole requires acknowledging how fragile the current structure is. Leadership does not always want to surface that vulnerability — especially founders who may not recognize that their departure would constitute an organizational crisis.

The audit is not complicated. Start with a simple question: if this person left tomorrow, what would we lose that is not written down anywhere? Do that for every senior staff member and every board member with an active external role. The answer tells you exactly where your organizational risk lives. Then build from there — not by replacing the people, but by creating the systems that make their knowledge portable.

We are not talking about bureaucracy for its own sake. Process in service of mission is one of the most underrated tools in community-centered work. The org that builds it does not become less relational — it becomes more durable. And durability, in the end, is what the communities these organizations serve actually need.

The organizations that will still be doing meaningful work in Detroit and every other city twenty years from now are not the ones with the best networks today. They are the ones building systems that can carry relationships forward beyond any single person's tenure. That is a design problem, and design problems have solutions. The next question worth asking is who inside your organization is responsible for building that architecture — and whether they have the authority and the time to actually do it.
