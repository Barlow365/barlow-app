What Amazon HQ2 Taught Every City About Desperation

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
July 23, 2026 7 min read

In 2017, Amazon sent out a request for proposals and 238 cities responded. Two hundred and thirty-eight. Cities drafted tax incentive packages, commissioned renderings, filmed recruitment videos, and delivered the functional equivalent of a cover letter for a job they weren't sure they wanted. The winning bid — Northern Virginia — came with an estimated $750 million in state and local incentives. What we learned from the whole exercise wasn't about Amazon. It was about how broken our civic economic development instincts actually are.

The Structure of the Trap

Amazon didn't create the desperation. It just built a machine that revealed it at scale. The HQ2 search was, structurally, a reverse auction — except instead of driving prices down, it drove public subsidies up. Cities that understood this declined to participate. Most didn't understand it, or understood it and participated anyway because the political cost of sitting out felt higher than the financial cost of losing.

That's the trap. When economic development becomes a competition for headlines, the incentives shift away from building durable local capacity and toward performing urgency. The RFP becomes the goal. The press release becomes the strategy. And somewhere in the process, the actual question — what does our city actually need to grow its tax base, employ its residents, and build long-term fiscal health — gets deferred.

Detroit went through its own version of this cycle for decades before the bankruptcy forced a different conversation. The city spent years chasing deals, offering breaks, clearing land for projects that either didn't materialize or didn't produce the downstream benefits promised. It wasn't incompetence. It was the logical output of a system that rewarded announcements over outcomes.

What the Bidding War Numbers Actually Show

The Good Jobs First subsidy tracker documented that Amazon had already received over $4.1 billion in state and local subsidies across its operations before HQ2 even launched. The company knew how to extract public value. Cities knew this and participated anyway, because the frame was wrong from the start. They were asking 'how do we win Amazon' instead of 'what does winning actually mean for our residents.'

According to a 2018 analysis by economic development researchers at the Upjohn Institute, the average subsidy per job in large corporate relocation deals is often north of $150,000 — and the promised jobs frequently arrive years late, at lower numbers, and with weaker wage floors than the original projections. The math rarely closes the way the press conference suggests it will.

This isn't an argument against economic development incentives as a tool. It's a diagnosis of what happens when the tool becomes the policy. Incentives are supposed to tip marginal decisions — to make a company choose your city over an equally viable competitor when they were already coming to your region. They are not supposed to substitute for a city having something worth choosing in the first place.

What Pittsburgh Did Differently

Pittsburgh is worth studying here, not because it's a perfect model, but because it represents a different posture. When Pittsburgh's tech and medical sector grew over the past two decades, the growth was largely anchored to Carnegie Mellon, the University of Pittsburgh, and the talent pipelines those institutions created. Amazon actually opened a technology development center in Pittsburgh — not because of an HQ2-style incentive war, but because the talent was already there.

That's a different kind of leverage. When a city has something specific, something that requires years to build and can't be replicated by a competing city's check, it negotiates from a different position. Pittsburgh didn't need to perform desperation because it had built something real. The distinction matters: Pittsburgh invested in its own capacity first, and the corporate interest followed.

Detroit is now attempting a version of this. The investment in Corktown, the mobility corridor work, the partnerships with Wayne State and University of Michigan — these are attempts to build a specific, defensible economic identity around mobility and manufacturing technology. It's slower than a headline deal. It's also more durable.

The Visibility Problem in Civic Competition

One reason cities keep falling into this pattern is that the alternative is invisible. When a city invests in workforce training, improved transit corridors, or small business ecosystem development, the results are diffuse, delayed, and hard to attribute. When a city lands a corporate headquarters, the ribbon-cutting is on the front page.

Elected officials and economic development offices operate inside political cycles that punish patience. A mayor who spends three years building the conditions for organic small business growth and sees meaningful results in year five gets no credit at year two. A mayor who signs a headline deal in month eight gets a reelection bump whether or not the deal produces.

This isn't a character flaw. It's a structural incentive problem. The fix requires changing what we measure and when we measure it — not just lecturing cities about short-termism. If we want cities to behave differently, we have to redesign the accountability systems around economic development outcomes, not economic development announcements. That's a harder problem than any individual RFP response.

What a Non-Desperate City Looks Like

A city that isn't desperate knows what it has. It has mapped its actual assets — workforce skill concentrations, physical infrastructure, institutional anchors, cultural identity — and it has a theory of growth that connects those assets to market opportunities. It makes targeted plays instead of responding to every RFP that crosses a staffer's desk.

Non-desperate cities also say no. They decline deals that would extract more than they contribute. They push back on subsidy requests from companies that are going to locate in the region regardless. They use incentives surgically, for deals that genuinely turn on the marginal cost, not as a default negotiating posture.

The HQ2 process gave us a clean data set: 238 cities revealed their bidding behavior under pressure, and most of them revealed that they don't have a coherent theory of what they're building toward. Amazon got a great deal. Cities got a lesson. Whether they learn from it is a different question — and the answer, city by city, will shape the next twenty years of American urban economic development more than any single headquarters announcement ever could.

The Amazon HQ2 story isn't really about Amazon. It's a diagnostic. It showed us which cities have a strategy and which cities have a posture. It showed us where economic development is a system and where it's a reflex. Detroit, Pittsburgh, and every other city rebuilding from a difficult decade faces the same core question: are we building something specific and durable, or are we just very good at responding to RFPs? The answer determines everything that follows — and we'll be looking at the mechanics of what 'building something specific' actually requires in the next piece.
