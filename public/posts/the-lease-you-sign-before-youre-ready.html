The Lease You Sign Before You're Ready

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
September 22, 2026 7 min read

About 20% of small businesses fail within their first year. By year five, roughly half are gone. We talk about that number like it's a personality flaw — the owner wasn't tough enough, disciplined enough, hungry enough. But when you look at the wreckage up close, one pattern keeps surfacing before the others: the lease. Signed too soon, scoped too large, negotiated from the wrong position. The building didn't kill the business. The commitment to the building did.

The Pressure to Look Like You've Arrived

There's a particular kind of optimism that hits founders around month six or seven. Revenue is moving. Customers are real. The idea has graduated from a napkin to a functioning operation. And then someone — an investor, a mentor, a well-meaning cousin — says you need a real space. A place that signals legitimacy. A front door with your name on it.

That pressure is not imaginary. Storefronts do build credibility. Retail presence drives foot traffic. A commercial kitchen or workshop floor unlocks contracts that a home address never will. The problem isn't wanting space. The problem is confusing the appearance of scale with the infrastructure of scale. Those are two different things, and a five-year lease at $4,800 a month does not care which one you have.

In Detroit, we've watched this play out in neighborhood commercial corridors for years. A promising concept opens on Livernois or East Jefferson, signs a lease before the customer base is predictable, and spends the next eighteen months working for the building instead of building the business. The idea survives. The entity does not.

What the Lease Actually Commits You To

Most first-time operators read a commercial lease looking for the monthly number and the term. Those matter, but they're not where deals go wrong. The exposure is in the details that seem administrative until they aren't — personal guarantees, CAM charges, tenant improvement allowances with clawback provisions, exclusivity clauses, assignment restrictions.

A personal guarantee on a commercial lease means the business failing doesn't end your obligation. The landlord can pursue your personal assets. According to the Small Business Administration, personal guarantees are standard in the majority of small business commercial leases, particularly for tenants without established credit history or two or more years of financials. For a first-location operator, that's almost everyone.

Common Area Maintenance charges — CAM — can add 15 to 30 percent on top of the base rent in retail and mixed-use properties. A $3,500 base rent becomes $4,200 to $4,500 all-in. That delta matters when you're projecting cash flow. Many operators budget for the number they negotiated and get surprised by the number they owe.

None of this is predatory on its own. Commercial real estate has its own logic, and landlords are running a system too. The disconnect is that most small business owners enter that system underprepared, without counsel, and in a hurry.

The building didn't kill the business. The commitment to the building did.

The Cities That Show the Pattern Clearly

Detroit is useful for this analysis because the commercial real estate landscape is unusually legible here. Vacancy is visible. Turnover is traceable. You can drive down a corridor and count how many concepts have cycled through a single storefront in four years. Each one left something behind — a sign ghost, a coat of paint, a story someone tells at a dinner party about how the neighborhood just wasn't ready.

Cleveland's near-west commercial corridors show a similar pattern. Strong neighborhood identity, committed founders, community support — and still, a cycle of closures that cluster around lease renewal windows. The businesses that survive tend to share a characteristic: they didn't sign for the full space on day one. They started in shared kitchens, incubator spaces, or pop-up arrangements and graduated into permanent footprints only after proving unit economics.

Chicago's Pilsen and Bronzeville neighborhoods have produced enough case studies that community development organizations there now run lease literacy workshops as a precondition for certain small business grants. That's a system responding to a documented failure mode. It shouldn't require a grant program to trigger that education, but the fact that it exists tells you something about how widespread the problem is.

What Ready Actually Looks Like

There's no universal threshold, but there are diagnostics. Before signing a commercial lease, you should be able to answer three questions without hesitation: What is your break-even monthly revenue at full lease cost, including CAM and utilities? How many months of operating reserve do you have if revenue comes in 30 percent below projection? And have you run 12 months of actual revenue data, not projections?

If the answer to question three is no, you are not signing a lease from a position of knowledge. You are signing from a position of belief. Belief is not nothing — conviction matters in early-stage businesses. But conviction does not negotiate rent abatement or survive a slow January.

The operators who get this right tend to treat the lease search the same way they treat a hire. They define the requirements before they start looking. They set a hard budget ceiling at 10 to 12 percent of projected revenue, not projected best-case revenue. They engage a tenant's representative broker, who is compensated by the landlord and costs the tenant nothing, rather than negotiating against the landlord's agent alone. And they read the document with an attorney who does commercial real estate, not a general practice friend doing them a favor.

The System Isn't Designed to Slow You Down

Commercial real estate is not structured to protect first-time tenants. It doesn't need to be — that's not its function. The broker wants the deal closed. The landlord wants the vacancy filled. The lawyer on the other side of the table has done this transaction three hundred times. Nobody in that room is accountable for whether your business survives year three.

That accountability sits entirely with you. Which means the preparation has to happen before the room. Before the showing, before the letter of intent, before the excitement of a space that feels right. Feeling right is data, but it is not the only data.

According to SCORE, 82 percent of small business failures are tied to cash flow problems. A lease that consumes too much fixed overhead is not a cash flow problem in the abstract — it is a structural constraint that limits every decision you make afterward. You can't hire the right person. You can't weather a slow season. You can't take a calculated risk on a new product line because the building already owns your margin.

The fix here is not complicated. It is slow. Understand your numbers before you commit to a number. Get representation. Read what you sign. Build the business to the point where the lease is a tool, not a lifeline.

The lease is one node in a larger system — the same system that includes your pricing, your staffing model, your revenue concentration, and your relationship to capital. Fix any one of those in isolation and you're still operating with exposure. The operators who build something durable tend to see these decisions as connected, not sequential. The lease question leads directly to the capital structure question, which leads to the revenue model question. We'll get to those. But the lease is often where the damage starts, and it's worth treating it that way.
