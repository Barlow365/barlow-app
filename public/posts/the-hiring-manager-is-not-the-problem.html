The Hiring Manager Is Not the Problem

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
September 26, 2026 7 min read

Every few months, a new report comes out telling us that hiring managers are biased, slow, or out of touch. And every few months, HR teams run another training, update another rubric, and wonder why the outcomes don't change. Here is what those reports consistently miss: according to the Society for Human Resource Management, the average cost-per-hire in the U.S. is $4,700 — and that number climbs past $28,000 when you factor in lost productivity and onboarding. We are spending enormous resources trying to fix the person holding the clipboard, when the clipboard itself is the problem.

What the Hiring Manager Actually Inherits

When a hiring manager posts a role, they are not starting from a blank slate. They are inheriting a job description written by a committee three years ago, a salary band set by compensation consultants who have not looked at the local market since before inflation spiked, an ATS that filters out candidates before any human sees them, and a timeline dictated by a headcount freeze that just lifted. They did not build that system. They operate inside it.

This is the diagnostic error we keep making. We observe the output — a slow hire, a bad culture fit, a team that still lacks diversity after years of stated commitment — and we trace the failure to the last human who touched the process. That human is visible. The system upstream is not. So we train the human and leave the system alone.

In Detroit, where workforce development organizations have spent over a decade trying to connect residents to manufacturing and tech jobs, the friction point is rarely an individual hiring manager who does not want to hire locally. The friction is credential requirements written for a different labor market, background check policies that disqualify people before a conversation happens, and internal approval chains that move slower than candidates do. The manager wants to hire. The system makes it expensive to close.

The System Has Layers — Most of Them Are Invisible

Think of the hiring process as a pipeline with multiple pressure points. The job description layer filters by keyword. The ATS layer filters by format. The screening layer filters by first impression. The panel layer filters by consensus, which usually means comfort. By the time a candidate reaches a hiring manager for a substantive conversation, the field has already been narrowed by algorithms and assumptions the manager never set and probably cannot override.

A 2021 Harvard Business School study found that automated resume screening systems rejected millions of qualified applicants — people they termed 'hidden workers' — because those applicants had resume gaps, non-linear career paths, or unconventional credentials. The researchers estimated that 27 million Americans fell into this category. The hiring manager never saw them. The system decided.

Chicago has been running targeted workforce initiatives in its South and West sides for years, trying to move residents into living-wage jobs. The programs produce job-ready candidates. The pipeline stalls not at the community organization, not at the hiring manager, but at the interface between those two worlds — the application portal, the background screening vendor, the automated scheduling tool that sends candidates a link and never follows up. Layers of friction that no single person owns and therefore no single person fixes.

The hiring manager is not the lever. They are the indicator — and we keep adjusting the indicator instead of the machine.

Why We Keep Blaming Individuals Anyway

Individuals are easier to hold accountable than systems. You can put a name on a manager. You can measure their time-to-fill. You can send them to unconscious bias training and check a box. Systems do not have names. They have vendors, legacy configurations, legal review cycles, and budget owners spread across three departments. Accountability for a system requires political will and cross-functional authority that most organizations have not built.

There is also an incentive problem. The people who could change the system — C-suite leaders, HR architects, procurement teams — are rarely the ones who feel the daily pain of a broken process. The hiring manager feels it. The candidate feels it acutely. But the people with system-level access are often insulated from the operational consequences of bad design. So the feedback loop that should drive change never closes.

We have seen this pattern in Pittsburgh, where healthcare systems spent years complaining about nursing shortages while maintaining credential and scheduling requirements that made the jobs structurally inaccessible to the community members most willing to do them. The managers on the floor knew what needed to change. The system that governed hiring was managed three levels above them.

What a Systems Diagnosis Actually Looks Like

A real diagnosis does not start with the hiring manager's behavior. It starts with mapping the full pipeline and identifying where candidates drop out, where time accumulates, and where decisions get made without human judgment. Most organizations have never done this mapping. They assume the process works until the results prove otherwise, and then they look for a person to blame.

The questions worth asking are structural: Who wrote the job description, and when? What does the ATS reject automatically, and why? What is the average days-to-offer, broken down by role level and department? What percentage of candidates who make it to a hiring manager interview receive an offer? If that conversion rate is low, is the problem the manager's judgment or the quality of candidates the system is surfacing? These are engineering questions. They require data, not sensitivity training.

According to LinkedIn's 2023 Future of Recruiting report, 73 percent of talent professionals say skills-based hiring is a priority — but fewer than half have actually restructured their processes to reflect it. The intention is present. The system has not caught up. That gap between stated priority and operational reality is where most workforce problems actually live.

The Fix Requires Access to the Right Level

Hiring managers need defenders, not more auditors. The most useful thing an organization can do is give the people closest to the hiring process a legitimate channel to surface system-level problems — and ensure those problems reach decision-makers who can actually act on them. That is not a culture intervention. It is a governance question.

In Detroit, the organizations doing this well are the ones that have built direct relationships between employer HR leadership and workforce development intermediaries — not just at the program level, but at the policy level. They are renegotiating what credentials are required and why. They are piloting shorter application flows. They are measuring drop-off and treating it like a product metric. That is systems thinking applied to a social problem.

The hiring manager is not the lever. They are the indicator. When we see consistent hiring failures across managers, across departments, across time — that is a signal worth reading carefully. The system is telling us something. The question is whether we are positioned to hear it and act at the right altitude.

The workforce conversation in this country keeps circling the same drain because we keep solving at the wrong level. Individual behavior change inside a broken system produces incremental results at best. The organizations and cities making real progress — in Detroit, in Chicago, in communities everywhere trying to connect residents to economic opportunity — are the ones treating hiring as an engineered process with diagnosable failure points, not a human drama with villains to identify. The next article in this series will look at what skills-based hiring actually requires operationally — not the concept, but the plumbing.
