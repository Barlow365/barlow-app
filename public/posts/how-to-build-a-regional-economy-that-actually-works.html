How to Build a Regional Economy That Actually Works

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
June 13, 2026 5 min read

The Cleveland metropolitan area has 51 separate municipalities competing for the same pool of businesses and residents. Each maintains its own economic development office, offers overlapping incentive packages, and duplicates infrastructure investments. The result: a region with 2.1 million people that generates less economic output per capita than metro areas half its size. We've confused competition with effectiveness.

The Infrastructure Foundation

Regional economies start with shared infrastructure that no single municipality can build alone. Detroit's regional water system serves 4 million people across three counties because water doesn't respect city boundaries. The system works because it was designed as regional infrastructure from the beginning—not 40 separate utilities trying to coordinate after the fact.

Transportation networks follow the same logic. The most successful regional economies move people and goods efficiently across municipal lines. Dallas-Fort Worth built its DART rail system to connect employment centers in different cities. Workers live in one municipality and commute to jobs in another without thinking about it. That's how labor markets actually function.

We keep building infrastructure city by city, then wonder why our regions underperform. A manufacturer choosing between regions doesn't care about individual city boundaries. They care about workforce access, supply chain connectivity, and regulatory predictability across the entire area where they'll operate.

Workforce Systems That Scale

The most effective regional economies treat workforce development as a shared asset. Pittsburgh's region coordinates training programs across universities, community colleges, and trade schools. When a major employer needs specific skills, the entire regional education system responds—not just the institution that happens to be in the same municipality.

Detroit's regional approach to automotive workforce development shows what's possible. The Southeast Michigan region coordinates apprenticeship programs across multiple counties. Ford doesn't train workers just for Ford—they train workers for the entire automotive ecosystem. Suppliers, competitors, and support companies all benefit from the same talent pipeline.

Skills-based immigration follows similar patterns. Regions that coordinate visa sponsorship and talent attraction outcompete those where individual cities fight over the same limited pool of workers. We're seeing this play out in real time as remote work redistributes talent across regions.

We've confused competition with effectiveness—the strongest regional economies coordinate their core systems while competing on execution.

Coordinated Economic Development

The most functional regional economies have learned to coordinate rather than cannibalize. When one municipality offers a company a tax break, neighboring cities don't automatically match it. They've agreed on regional standards that prevent race-to-the-bottom incentive wars.

Denver's metro area operates this way. The region coordinates major economic development decisions and shares tax revenue from large projects. When Amazon was scouting for HQ2, the Denver region submitted a coordinated proposal instead of having individual cities compete against each other.

This requires giving up some local control in exchange for regional strength. Most municipalities resist this trade-off until they're forced to make it by fiscal crisis or economic decline. The regions that coordinate voluntarily before crisis hits tend to maintain their competitive advantages longer.

Revenue Sharing That Works

Tax base sharing sounds like socialist policy to many American ears, but it's actually how the most capitalist regions maintain their competitive edge. The Minneapolis-St. Paul metro area shares 40% of commercial and industrial tax growth across the region. Cities still compete for residents and businesses, but they don't compete by starving their neighbors of resources.

This prevents the hollowing-out dynamic that weakens entire regions. When businesses move from inner-ring suburbs to greenfield developments at the metropolitan edge, the region maintains its overall tax base instead of subsidizing endless sprawl with diminishing returns.

Detroit's regional transit authority operates on a similar principle—funding comes from the entire region because the system serves the entire region. Individual municipalities can't opt out of regional systems while still benefiting from regional economic growth.

Governance That Scales

Regional economic development requires governance structures that can make binding decisions across municipal boundaries. This doesn't mean eliminating local government—it means creating regional capacity for issues that transcend local boundaries.

Portland's regional government model demonstrates one approach. Metro has authority over land use planning, transportation, and economic development for three counties and 25 cities. Local municipalities maintain control over local issues, but regional issues get handled at regional scale.

The alternative is what we see in most American metros: 50+ separate jurisdictions trying to coordinate through voluntary cooperation and informal agreements. This works for small decisions but fails when regions need to make large, coordinated investments or policy changes.

Building regional economies that work requires us to think beyond municipal boundaries without eliminating local control. The regions that figure this out first will have decisive advantages in attracting investment, developing talent, and maintaining quality of life. The ones that don't will keep wondering why their individual cities are working harder but their regions are falling behind. Regional coordination isn't about giving up local identity—it's about building the foundation that makes local success possible.
