The Permit Is Not the Problem

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
July 30, 2026 7 min read

The average single-family permit in the United States takes between 3 and 6 months to clear, depending on jurisdiction. Developers cite that number constantly, and they are not wrong to flag it. But when a project dies at month nine, after the permit was already in hand, we need to stop blaming the permit office and start reading the actual autopsy.

What the Permit Actually Measures

A permit is a checkpoint, not a conveyor belt. It tells you that a set of drawings met code on a specific day. What it does not tell you is whether the utility hookup is scheduled, whether the subcontractors are available, whether the construction loan still pencils at current interest rates, or whether the title chain is clean enough to close. Those variables operate on entirely different clocks, governed by entirely different systems.

When we treat permit velocity as the primary metric for housing production, we are measuring the one thing that is actually somewhat predictable and ignoring the four or five things that are not. That is a comfortable mistake because permit timelines are visible, trackable, and easy to complain about in a city council meeting. The rest of the machinery is harder to diagram.

According to the National Association of Home Builders, regulatory costs account for roughly 24 percent of the final price of a new single-family home. That figure includes zoning approvals, impact fees, inspections, and compliance overhead — not just the permit itself. If we zeroed out permit processing time entirely, we would still be working through roughly three-quarters of the regulatory cost burden. The permit is one node in a much longer chain.

Detroit and the Infrastructure Gap

Detroit is a useful case study because it has done a significant amount of work to modernize its permitting systems over the last decade. The Buildings, Safety Engineering and Environmental Department has reduced turnaround times and moved toward digital intake. Those improvements are real and worth acknowledging. But the city still loses development deals regularly — not because of permit delays, but because the infrastructure required to serve a new building does not exist or has not been maintained.

Water and sewer capacity in neighborhoods that lost population for fifty years was not designed for new dense infill. When a developer pulls a permit for a twelve-unit building on the east side, they can sometimes wait eighteen months for DTE to bring the electrical service up to the property line. That is not a permitting problem. That is a utility coordination problem, and it sits entirely outside the permit office's jurisdiction.

This pattern appears in legacy cities across the Midwest. In Cleveland, a 2022 study by the Urban Land Institute found that infrastructure readiness — not zoning or permitting — was cited by developers as the top barrier to infill residential projects in disinvested neighborhoods. The pipe is old. The transformer is undersized. The road needs rebuilding before the first shovel goes in. The permit was ready in sixty days. The project took three years.

We can streamline approvals in eighteen months through administrative reform. We cannot produce ten thousand new tradespeople in the same window.

The Financing Layer Nobody Wants to Talk About

Construction lending for small and mid-size projects has contracted sharply since 2022. The Federal Reserve's rate cycle pushed construction loan interest rates above eight percent for much of 2023, and community banks — which traditionally carry the smaller deals that larger institutions ignore — pulled back on new originations. A project that was underwritten at a five-percent construction rate and a six-percent permanent rate stopped working at eight and nine. The permit did not change. The numbers changed.

This is where the narrative around permitting reform gets genuinely misleading. Advocates for streamlining approvals are not wrong in their diagnosis of bureaucratic friction. But when the capital markets are the constraint, faster permits do not produce more housing. They produce faster approvals that sit on a shelf while the developer tries to find financing that no longer exists at the terms required to make the deal close.

Houston is frequently held up as the model for permissive land use and fast approvals. And Houston does build a lot of housing relative to most American cities. But even in Houston, production slowed meaningfully in 2023 as financing costs rose. The city did not change its permitting process. The interest rate environment did. That context matters when we are designing policy responses.

Workforce Capacity Is a Hard Ceiling

The construction industry is short roughly half a million workers, according to the Associated Builders and Contractors. That is not a projection — that is a current gap measured against existing demand. When a project has its permit, its financing, and its infrastructure ready, it still needs framers, plumbers, electricians, and concrete finishers who are available in that geography at that time.

In Detroit, the Detroit Building Authority and several workforce development partners have been working on skilled trades pipelines for years. Progress is real but slow, because training a journeyman electrician takes four years, not four months. The timeline for building workforce capacity does not match the timeline for cutting permit approvals. We can streamline approvals in eighteen months through administrative reform. We cannot produce ten thousand new tradespeople in the same window.

This is a systems problem. The inputs to housing production are interdependent, and they move at different speeds. Optimizing one input in isolation does not accelerate output — it just shifts where the bottleneck sits. We approve faster, and the project stalls waiting for a plumber. We train more plumbers, and the project stalls waiting for a construction loan. The system has to be read as a system.

What Builders Actually Need

The builders who are producing housing at scale — not just talking about it — are the ones who understand all five layers simultaneously. They are not waiting for a single policy fix. They are building relationships with utility companies before they pull permits. They are locking in financing structures that can absorb rate movement. They are maintaining rosters of subcontractors across multiple cities so they can shift capacity when one market slows.

That is a multi-system operator skill set. It requires knowing that the permit office is one room in a much larger building, and that the building itself has to be navigated, not just the front door.

Policy reform matters. Permit streamlining matters. Zoning modernization matters. But none of those levers, pulled alone, produces housing at the scale and cost that the current shortage requires. The fix is not faster approvals. The fix is a coordinated understanding of where the actual constraints live — and the discipline to work on all of them at once, even when the one that is easiest to measure is not the one that is actually binding.

Every city that is serious about housing production eventually arrives at the same uncomfortable realization: the permit was never the problem. The problem is the coordination failure that surrounds the permit — the utility that moves on its own schedule, the capital market that reprices overnight, the workforce that takes a generation to rebuild. That is harder to fix than a form. It is also the only fix that actually works. Next, we look at what a city that has gotten serious about coordinating those layers actually does differently.
