The Second Job Nobody Accounts For: Managing the Systems Around Your Work

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
September 17, 2026 8 min read

The average American spends roughly 8 hours per year on the phone with health insurance companies alone — not counting the time spent decoding explanation-of-benefits statements, disputing bills, or resubmitting claims that got lost in the system. That number comes from a 2022 survey by the Employee Benefit Research Institute, and it doesn't account for retirement account management, childcare coordination, or the 40 minutes most people spend on hold before they even talk to a human. That work is real. It takes real time and real cognitive load. And nobody built it into your job description.

The Work Behind the Work

We talk a lot about job quality — wages, benefits, advancement. What we talk about less is the administrative tax that sits on top of every job. This is the scheduling of doctors appointments during business hours. The insurance portal that rejects your login. The FSA deadline you missed because no one told you it existed. The 401(k) rollover that's been sitting in limbo since you left your last employer two years ago.

This isn't a personal failing. It's a structural one. The systems that surround employment — benefits administration, healthcare navigation, financial services, childcare access — were largely designed in isolation from each other, and none of them were designed with your actual schedule in mind. The result is a second job that nobody hired you for and nobody pays you to do.

In Detroit, where a significant portion of the workforce is employed in shift-based manufacturing, logistics, and healthcare support roles, this problem is acute. If your shift starts at 6 a.m. and ends at 2 p.m., you can technically make a 3 p.m. call to your insurance carrier. But you're doing it on the drive home, on a cell phone, exhausted, while navigating I-75. That's not a functioning system. That's a system that assumes you have flexibility you don't have.

What the Numbers Actually Show

A 2021 report from the National Alliance of Healthcare Purchaser Coalitions found that employees lose an average of $1,400 per year in productivity and personal time managing healthcare administration — and that figure underestimates the cost for people in hourly roles, where time away from the floor is time not paid. When you extend that logic to the full cluster of systems a person manages — not just healthcare but housing applications, utility programs, SNAP recertification, childcare subsidies — the number compounds fast.

Harvard Business Review published research in 2019 showing that administrative burden in healthcare alone contributes to burnout at a rate comparable to clinical workload. Physicians get the headline, but the same cognitive drain hits anybody navigating a system that wasn't built for efficiency. The person trying to recertify for a childcare assistance program while working doubles at a distribution center in Memphis isn't experiencing something categorically different from a doctor drowning in prior authorizations. The mechanism is the same: a system that extracts time and attention as a condition of participation.

This is one reason workforce retention remains stubbornly difficult even when wages rise. You can increase someone's hourly rate by two dollars and still lose them if the surrounding systems are costing them three hours a week in unpaid administrative labor. The job is harder than the job description suggests, and workers know it even when they can't fully articulate why.

Where Cities Are Getting This Wrong — and Occasionally Right

Detroit has made serious investments in workforce development over the last decade. Detroit at Work, the city's workforce development initiative, connects residents to training programs, job placement, and employer partnerships. It's a real infrastructure effort. But the gap between placing someone in a job and helping them stay in a job is still wide, and a lot of what falls into that gap is the administrative overhead we're describing.

Chicago has experimented with benefits navigation as a wraparound service inside workforce development programs — essentially embedding someone whose job is to help workers manage the systems around their employment. The results are modest but measurable: retention rates improve when people have help navigating open enrollment, resolving billing errors, or accessing childcare subsidies they qualify for but didn't know existed. It's not a solution. It's a patch. But it points at the real problem.

The more durable fix isn't hiring navigators to manage broken systems — it's building systems that don't require navigation. That means benefits portals that work on mobile. Recertification processes that don't expire on arbitrary deadlines. Childcare subsidy programs that don't require a full day off work to apply for. These aren't radical design requirements. They're the baseline for a system built around the people using it rather than the people administering it.

What Employers Are Actually Responsible For

There's a tendency to frame this as a government problem — and some of it is. But employers carry more responsibility here than most acknowledge. Open enrollment is a case study in institutional indifference. Most companies send a PDF, schedule a one-hour Zoom that half the workforce can't attend, and call it communication. Then they're surprised when employees make suboptimal benefit elections that cost them money all year, reduce their engagement, and eventually drive them to look for work somewhere else.

The employers who are doing this well have stopped treating benefits communication as a compliance checkbox. They're building year-round touchpoints. They're using text-based outreach for shift workers who don't live in their email inbox. They're running benefits fairs during shift transitions rather than during standard business hours. These aren't expensive interventions. They require operational intention, not budget.

The same applies to retirement. Forty-five percent of private-sector workers have no access to an employer-sponsored retirement plan, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. But even among those who do, participation rates in lower-wage brackets remain low — often because the enrollment process requires attention and paperwork that workers simply don't have bandwidth for during the hiring process. Auto-enrollment with opt-out provisions has shown consistent improvement in participation. Most employers still haven't implemented it.

Building Around the Real Schedule

What we're really talking about is a design problem. Every system that surrounds a job — healthcare, retirement, childcare, housing assistance, transportation benefits — was designed with assumptions about how a worker moves through the world. Those assumptions are often wrong, and they're especially wrong for the workers who need those systems most.

The builder's job is to look at the actual load people are carrying, not the theoretical one. When we design workforce programs, benefits platforms, or employer onboarding processes, we have to account for the fact that our users are already managing a second job we didn't write into the contract. If we ignore that, we're not building a service. We're adding to the burden.

Detroit has the workforce, the institutional infrastructure, and frankly the urgency to lead on this. So does every mid-sized industrial city watching retention problems persist despite genuine wage growth. The question isn't whether the administrative tax is real. It is real. The question is whether we're willing to build around it — or whether we keep designing systems for workers who have more time and flexibility than most workers actually have.

The second job nobody accounts for is costing workers time, money, and energy they don't have to spare — and it's costing employers retention they can't afford to lose. The fix isn't a navigator or a one-page FAQ. It's treating administrative burden as a design constraint from the start. Next, we'll look at how a few employers and city programs are restructuring onboarding specifically to reduce the overhead workers carry in the first 90 days — and what the data shows about whether it actually moves the needle on retention.
