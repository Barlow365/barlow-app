The Advisory Board That Never Advises Anyone

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
September 24, 2026 7 min read

According to BoardSource's 2021 Leading with Intent report, only 27% of nonprofit board members say their board is effective at setting organizational strategy. That number should stop everyone in the room. We have built an entire civic and nonprofit infrastructure around a governance structure that, by its own participants' admission, mostly does not work. And yet the advisory board — that collection of prominent names on a letterhead — remains one of the most reliably replicated features of organizational life in Detroit, Cleveland, and cities across the country. We keep building them. We rarely ask what they're actually for.

What Advisory Boards Are Actually Doing

Most advisory boards exist to solve a perception problem, not an operational one. A new nonprofit needs credibility. A startup needs to signal legitimacy to funders. A city initiative needs the appearance of community buy-in. So someone assembles a list of respected people — a retired executive, a university dean, a well-known pastor, a couple of business owners — puts their names on the website, and calls it an advisory board. The organization gets a credibility halo. The advisors get an affiliation that looks good on their own bios. Everyone shakes hands.

The problem is not that this is cynical. Most of the time it is not cynical at all. It is earnest. The founders genuinely believe the advisors will help. The advisors genuinely intend to. But the structure is rarely built to produce anything, which means good intentions hit a wall of ambiguity. Nobody defined the ask. Nobody set a meeting cadence that respects anyone's time. Nobody built a feedback loop between what the advisors know and what the organization actually decides. The board becomes a formality, and formalities drift toward irrelevance.

The Real Cost of Decorative Governance

There is a cost here beyond wasted potential. When advisory boards carry no real function, they absorb credibility without producing accountability. The organization gets to say it has community input. Funders see diverse, impressive names and check a box. But the people on that board — many of whom are leaders in their own right, with real knowledge of the neighborhoods and systems in question — are sidelined by a structure that never actually asked them anything.

In Detroit, this pattern shows up across sectors. Workforce development organizations, place-based initiatives, and civic tech projects all tend to build advisory structures that meet twice a year, review a slide deck, and offer no binding input on anything. The same dynamic plays out in cities like Baltimore, where community advisory boards attached to hospital systems have been repeatedly criticized for lacking real authority over decisions that affect the neighborhoods those hospitals sit inside. The board exists. The influence does not.

A 2019 study published in Nonprofit Management and Leadership found that boards with clearly defined roles and regular engagement produced measurably stronger organizational outcomes — not a surprising finding, but a damning one given how rarely those conditions are actually met. We know what works. We keep building the other thing.

If you cannot explain what the board will change about how you operate, you are not building governance — you are managing appearances.

Why the Structure Persists

Advisory boards persist because they are cheap to assemble and difficult to criticize. Saying you have an advisory board is safer than saying you make decisions alone. It creates a buffer. If a program fails, the failure is distributed. If a community complains it was not consulted, there is a board to point to. The structure offers protection without demanding performance.

There is also a social dynamic that nobody talks about openly. Inviting someone to an advisory board is a form of relationship maintenance. It is a way to honor a connection, to keep someone in your orbit, to signal mutual respect. That is not inherently wrong. Relationships matter in organizational work. But when relationship maintenance becomes the primary function of a governance structure, the governance disappears. You end up with a very pleasant email list.

What a Functional Advisory Structure Actually Looks Like

The fix is not complicated, but it requires honesty about what you actually need. Before inviting anyone to an advisory board, the organization has to answer three questions: What specific decisions or functions do we need outside expertise to inform? What is the realistic time commitment we can ask of people with full lives? And what will we do differently because of what they tell us? If you cannot answer all three, you are not ready to build an advisory board. You are ready to build a mailing list.

Functional advisory structures tend to be smaller, more focused, and time-limited. A six-person advisory group convened for a specific product or program decision, with a defined engagement window of four to six months, will almost always outperform a twelve-person annual board with a vague mandate. The people on it know what they're there for. They can see whether their input is being used. They have a reason to stay engaged.

In practical terms, this means restructuring the ask. Instead of 'will you join our advisory board,' the conversation becomes 'we are making a decision about X over the next quarter, and your expertise in Y is directly relevant — can we meet with you three times and run our thinking by you?' That is a cleaner request. It respects the advisor's time. It creates a concrete exchange. And it produces something the organization can actually use.

Minneapolis has experimented with this model in parts of its community development sector, embedding subject-matter advisors into specific program design phases rather than standing up permanent boards. The results are not universal, but the principle holds: specificity creates accountability, and accountability creates output.

The Harder Conversation

Some organizations need to dissolve the advisory boards they already have. That is uncomfortable to say out loud, but it is true. If a board has not produced a documented recommendation, a substantive critique, or a decision-relevant insight in the past twelve months, it is not an advisory board. It is a list. Maintaining it without reform is a form of organizational self-deception that funders, staff, and community members eventually see through.

The harder conversation is about power. Advisory boards that have no authority tend to attract advisors who are comfortable with no authority. The people who might push back the hardest — who might name the real problems, challenge the theory of change, or redirect organizational resources — often opt out of toothless structures. They have seen enough of them. So the organizations that most need honest outside counsel end up with rooms full of people who are too polite, too busy, or too disconnected to deliver it.

This is a systems problem, not a character problem. The people running these organizations are not bad actors. The advisors who show up twice a year are not frauds. Everyone is operating inside a structure that was never designed to produce anything specific, and structures that produce nothing tend to calcify rather than improve. Fixing it means getting honest about what outside expertise is actually worth, what it costs other people to provide it, and what the organization is genuinely willing to change because of what it hears. Detroit has enough talent in its orbit to build advisory structures that actually move things. So do most cities. The question is whether organizations are willing to build something functional instead of something comfortable. That question comes before the invitations go out.
