Why Experience Should Be a Credential

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
June 11, 2026 4 min read

Only 16% of skilled trade workers have college degrees, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, yet these are the people who build our bridges, wire our buildings, and keep our cities running. Meanwhile, we continue structuring hiring and advancement around credentials that have little correlation with actual capability. We've built a system that values certificates over competence, degrees over delivery.

The Paper Trail Problem

Detroit's manufacturing comeback illustrates this disconnect perfectly. Ford's new electric vehicle facility required thousands of skilled positions, but traditional hiring filters would have screened out many of the workers who actually got the job done. The best assembly line supervisors we worked with had learned manufacturing through decades on the floor, not through supply chain management courses.

The credential obsession creates artificial barriers. A programmer who taught themselves through open-source contributions gets passed over for someone with a computer science degree but no shipped products. A facilities manager who has maintained complex systems for fifteen years loses out to an MBA with zero operational experience.

We're measuring inputs instead of outputs. The question isn't where someone learned to solve problems — it's whether they can solve them consistently and at scale.

What Experience Actually Teaches

Experience creates pattern recognition that no classroom can replicate. The electrician who has troubleshot thousands of electrical issues develops an intuitive sense for where problems hide. The project manager who has delivered fifty complex builds understands which risks matter and which don't.

More importantly, experience teaches failure recovery. Academic knowledge assumes perfect conditions. Real-world experience assumes nothing goes according to plan. The difference between theoretical knowledge and practical competence is learning how to adapt when systems break down.

In Chicago's infrastructure projects, the most effective supervisors aren't the ones with the best engineering credentials — they're the ones who have seen every type of failure mode and know how to keep projects moving when unexpected problems surface.

Experience creates pattern recognition that no classroom can replicate.

Building Better Assessment

Some organizations are already shifting toward competency-based evaluation. IBM eliminated degree requirements for many technical roles and saw no drop in performance quality. Google's data shows that academic performance has zero correlation with job success after two years of employment.

The military has always understood this principle. Promotion depends on demonstrated leadership under pressure, not classroom performance. They create structured ways to observe and measure what matters: decision-making under stress, team coordination, problem-solving with limited resources.

We need similar approaches in civilian employment. Portfolio-based assessment for creative roles. Practical demonstrations for technical positions. Problem-solving scenarios that reveal thinking patterns. These methods take more effort than scanning for degree requirements, but they identify actual capability.

The Practical Path Forward

Start with internal promotions. Many organizations already know who their effective operators are — they're just not systematically promoting them over credential holders. Create advancement paths based on demonstrated results rather than educational background.

Develop apprenticeship and mentorship programs that create structured experience. Rather than requiring four-year degrees for management roles, create two-year internal development tracks that combine hands-on responsibility with targeted learning.

In Phoenix, construction companies are experimenting with competency-based hiring that focuses on practical skills assessment. Candidates demonstrate their abilities through real work scenarios rather than answering questions about their education history. Early results show better job performance and lower turnover.

Measuring What Matters

Experience-based credentials need structure to be credible. Document specific achievements, not just years served. Track problem-solving instances, project outcomes, team leadership examples. Create portfolios that demonstrate capability progression over time.

Peer validation matters more than institutional validation. The respect of experienced practitioners carries more weight than academic grades. Build systems where skilled workers can vouch for each other's capabilities based on direct observation.

The goal isn't to eliminate education — it's to stop using education as a proxy for competence when better measures exist. Learning matters, but learning happens everywhere, not just in accredited institutions.

Credential inflation hurts everyone except credentialing institutions. Workers get locked out of opportunities despite proven ability. Organizations miss out on their most capable people. Cities lose economic mobility when practical skills get devalued. The fix isn't complicated — we just need to start measuring what actually predicts success rather than what's easiest to verify on paper.
