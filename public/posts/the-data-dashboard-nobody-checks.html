The Data Dashboard Nobody Checks

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
August 4, 2026 6 min read

According to a 2022 report from the National League of Cities, more than 70 percent of mid-to-large U.S. cities have launched some form of open data or performance dashboard initiative in the last decade. Detroit has one. Cleveland has one. Baltimore has one. Most of them are updated on a lag, buried three clicks deep on a municipal website, and consulted by exactly the people who built them. The data exists. The decisions don't change. That gap is not a technology problem.

We Built the Instrument Panel, Then Ignored It

There is a particular kind of organizational dysfunction where the measurement system and the decision-making system exist in parallel but never actually touch. You see it in corporations. You see it in nonprofits. You see it most visibly in city government, where dashboard culture exploded after the Bloomberg-era data-for-good movement convinced everyone that transparency plus metrics would equal accountability.

It did not. What it produced was a procurement cycle. Cities hired vendors, stood up platforms, held press events, and called it progress. Detroit's open data portal lists dozens of datasets — blight violations, 911 response times, demolition permits — all technically accessible. What it does not list is which department director reviewed which metric before which budget decision. That connection was never built into the system.

The dashboard was the deliverable. Behavior change was assumed to follow automatically. It rarely does.

The Difference Between Data and a Decision Trigger

A dashboard is not a decision system. It is a reporting system. The distinction matters more than most people want to admit, because conflating the two lets organizations feel like they are being rigorous when they are actually just being busy.

A decision system has triggers. It has owners. It has a defined cadence where someone with authority looks at a specific number and is required to respond to it — not comment on it, not archive it, but respond. A reporting system has none of that. It just displays. When vacancy rates in a neighborhood cross a threshold, who is notified? What protocol activates? In most cities, the answer is nothing automatic. A human has to choose to care that day.

In Pittsburgh, the city's performance management office — one of the more functional ones in the country — has made deliberate progress on this by tying specific metrics to quarterly leadership reviews with actual line authority. It is not perfect. But the structure acknowledges the basic truth that data does not act. People act, and people need structure to act consistently.

Most cities never got to that step. They got the portal and stopped.

The dashboard was the deliverable. Behavior change was assumed to follow automatically. It rarely does.

What Gets Measured Versus What Gets Managed

Peter Drucker's line — that you can't manage what you can't measure — has been inverted in practice. We now measure many things we have no intention of managing. The measurement becomes a form of performance itself, a signal that the organization is serious without requiring that it actually be serious.

Detroit has spent years working through the aftermath of municipal bankruptcy, rebuilding operational capacity across departments that were chronically under-resourced. That context matters. You cannot expect a department running at sixty percent staffing to also maintain a sophisticated data-review culture. The dashboard becomes one more unfunded mandate layered on top of people who are already making judgment calls under pressure every day.

The U.S. Government Accountability Office noted in a 2021 report on federal performance management that agencies most likely to use performance data in decision-making were those with dedicated staff assigned specifically to that function — not generalists asked to add it to existing responsibilities. Cities that treat data review as someone's side project should not be surprised when it stays that way.

The Vendor Problem Nobody Wants to Name

There is a supply-side dynamic here that deserves direct attention. The market for civic data platforms is not structured around outcomes. It is structured around deployments. A vendor gets paid to stand up the system. Renewal is tied to platform uptime and feature additions, not to whether anyone used the data to make a measurably better decision.

This is not a conspiracy. It is just misaligned incentives operating exactly as misaligned incentives always do. The vendor's job ends at delivery. The city's job — changing how its people actually work — begins there, and that is harder, slower, and more expensive than any technology purchase. It requires organizational development, not software.

We have watched this pattern repeat in Detroit, in Baltimore, in mid-size cities across the Midwest that chased the same smart-city playbook without the internal capacity to absorb it. The dashboards persist. The behavior stays the same. Then the next administration comes in, declares the old system inadequate, and procures a new one.

What a Functioning Version Actually Looks Like

The fix is not more dashboards. It is not better dashboards. It is designing decision systems where data is embedded into existing authority structures rather than displayed beside them and hoped for.

That means three things in practice. First, every tracked metric needs a named owner with actual decision authority — not a team, not a department, a person. Second, review cycles need to be calendared and non-negotiable, the same way budget reviews are. Third, the question on the table during those reviews cannot be 'what does this data show?' It has to be 'what are we doing differently because of this data?' Those are not the same question.

Some of this requires political will. Some of it requires protected staff capacity. All of it requires admitting that the dashboard launch was the beginning of the work, not the end. That admission is harder than it sounds in environments where press releases function as accountability and visibility functions as impact.

The cities that make this work will not do it by buying better software. They will do it by treating data review as an operational discipline with the same seriousness they give to payroll and permitting. Boring, necessary, and owned by someone whose job depends on it.

The deeper issue underneath all of this is that we have confused infrastructure with culture. You can build the pipeline without building the practice of drinking from it. Cities are not unique in this — it shows up in corporate analytics stacks, in nonprofit impact reporting, in any system where measurement got funded and application got assumed. The next question worth asking is not what data we should be collecting. It is what decisions we are actually willing to let data change. That answer tells you everything about whether any of this is real.
