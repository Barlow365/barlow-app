Why Neighborhoods Need CFOs, Not Just Activists

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
July 18, 2026 7 min read

There are roughly 40,000 community development organizations operating in the United States. Most of them are understaffed, undercapitalized, and built around a single charismatic leader whose departure would collapse the whole operation. According to the Urban Institute, less than 10 percent of community development corporations have a dedicated financial officer. That gap is not a personnel problem. It is a structural one — and it explains why so many neighborhoods stay stuck despite decades of genuine effort and real passion from people who care deeply about where they live.

The Passion Trap

Activism is how problems get named. Organizing is how pressure gets applied. Both matter. But neither one builds a balance sheet, manages a loan covenant, or structures a tax credit deal that keeps an affordable housing project solvent for thirty years. We have confused the act of caring about a neighborhood with the act of operating one — and communities are paying for that confusion in stalled projects, collapsed nonprofits, and unrealized potential.

Detroit is the clearest case study available. The city has produced some of the most committed community organizers in the country. It also watched hundreds of millions of dollars in philanthropic investment flow through organizations that lacked the financial infrastructure to deploy it efficiently. The Skillman Foundation, Kresge Foundation, and others have spent years trying to close that gap — not by replacing activists, but by building the operational layer underneath them. The lesson from Detroit is not that passion is wrong. It is that passion without financial architecture is incomplete.

Walk through any neighborhood in Baltimore or Cleveland that has cycled through multiple rounds of revitalization planning and you will find the same pattern. Reports written. Consultants paid. Ribbon cuttings held. Then, three years later, the anchor project is in receivership and the community land trust has no reserves. The diagnosis is almost always the same: the organization was built to advocate, not to operate.

What a Neighborhood CFO Actually Does

The title is a metaphor, but the function is literal. A neighborhood needs someone — whether that is a person, a team, or a shared services arrangement across multiple organizations — who is reading cash flows, stress-testing assumptions, structuring capital stacks, and asking hard questions about financial sustainability before a project breaks ground. That function does not exist in most community organizations because funders historically rewarded programs, not infrastructure.

Consider what financial leadership actually looks like at the neighborhood scale. It means knowing that a New Markets Tax Credit allocation has a seven-year compliance period and planning for what happens in year eight. It means understanding that a tax increment financing district generates revenue on a lag and cannot be used to cover operating shortfalls in year one. It means modeling what happens to a community development financial institution's loan portfolio when interest rates move 200 basis points. These are not exotic skills. They are standard financial operator competencies — and they are almost entirely absent from most neighborhood-level organizations.

In Detroit's Grandmont Rosedale neighborhood, the community development corporation built a genuine internal capacity for financial management over years of deliberate investment. The result was not just a healthier balance sheet. It was credibility with banks, developers, and city government that unlocked deals which advocacy alone could never have reached. That credibility is the product of operational discipline, not charisma.

We have confused the act of caring about a neighborhood with the act of operating one — and communities are paying for that confusion in stalled projects and unrealized potential.

The Funder Accountability Problem

We have to put some of this on funders. The philanthropic and government funding ecosystem has systematically underinvested in the financial infrastructure of community organizations while overinvesting in program delivery. A foundation will readily grant $500,000 for a workforce program and refuse to fund the $80,000 finance director position that would ensure the program's books are clean, its compliance requirements are met, and its outcomes are accurately measured. That is a structural incentive pointing in the wrong direction.

According to a 2020 report from the National Council of Nonprofits, only 20 percent of nonprofits maintain three or more months of operating reserves — the minimum threshold most financial advisors consider adequate for organizational stability. For community development organizations operating in distressed markets, that number is almost certainly lower. We are asking neighborhood-level operators to manage complex real estate transactions, federal grant compliance, and community accountability simultaneously, with no financial cushion and no dedicated financial leadership. Then we express surprise when things go sideways.

The shift required from funders is not dramatic. It is a reframe. General operating support, capacity-building grants, and shared CFO services are not overhead. They are the load-bearing infrastructure that makes every program dollar more effective. Chicago's LISC office has been one of the more intentional practitioners of this model — embedding financial technical assistance directly into its community development partnerships rather than treating it as an afterthought. The neighborhoods that have benefited show it in their deal flow.

Building the Operating Layer

The practical path forward is not for every block club to hire a CFO. That is neither realistic nor necessary. What is realistic is a shared services model where financial expertise is pooled across multiple neighborhood organizations, similar to how back-office functions get consolidated in other sectors. Several cities are experimenting with this, including Detroit through the work of organizations like Michigan Community Resources, which provides legal and financial capacity to small nonprofits that could not afford those services independently.

The other piece is leadership pipeline. We need people who understand both community development and financial operations — not one or the other. That means practitioners who can sit in a room with a tax credit syndicator in the morning and a block association in the evening and translate fluently between both. Those people exist, but we are not building systems to develop more of them at scale. Community development finance is still treated as a niche subspecialty rather than a core competency for anyone serious about neighborhood change.

There is also a governance dimension. Boards of community development organizations need to include people with genuine financial expertise — not just community representatives, not just well-meaning professionals from unrelated fields, but people who understand real estate finance, nonprofit accounting, and capital markets. Board composition is not a diversity checklist. It is a risk management function.

Passion Still Has a Job

None of this is an argument against organizers, advocates, or the people who show up at city council meetings at 10 p.m. on a Tuesday because they refuse to let their neighborhood be ignored. That work is essential. It sets the conditions under which financial operators can function — it creates political will, community trust, and the social license that no spreadsheet can manufacture.

The argument is about sequencing and structure, not replacement. Advocacy without financial architecture produces declarations. Financial architecture without community trust produces displacement. The neighborhoods that actually improve over time are the ones that figure out how to run both tracks simultaneously — the organizer and the operator working from the same table, toward the same goal, with shared accountability for both the vision and the numbers.

The next article in this series will look at specific financial structures — community land trusts, CDFIs, and patient capital vehicles — that give neighborhood operators the tools to actually hold ground once it is won. The financial architecture question does not end with hiring the right person. It extends into the instruments, the ownership structures, and the capital sources that determine whether neighborhood wealth stays in the neighborhood. That is the next layer of the system worth diagnosing.
