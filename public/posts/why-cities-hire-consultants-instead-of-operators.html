Why Cities Hire Consultants Instead of Operators

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
October 20, 2026 7 min read

The U.S. federal government and local municipalities spend an estimated $80 billion annually on outside consulting contracts. That number is not a scandal. It is a symptom. When you look at what cities actually buy with that money, the pattern is consistent: studies, frameworks, recommendations, and decks. Rarely do they buy someone who has run the thing they are trying to fix.

The Structural Reason This Happens

Cities are not stupid. The people running municipal procurement are working within a system that punishes operational risk and rewards process compliance. When a city hires a consultant and the project fails, the blame diffuses. The consultant wrote the report. The council approved it. The department implemented it. No single person is accountable, which means no single person gets fired. That diffusion of accountability is a feature of the system, not a bug.

When a city hires an operator — someone who actually runs housing, transit, workforce development, or economic development as a business — the accountability concentrates. That person makes decisions. Those decisions have outcomes. Outcomes are measurable. Measurable things can be used against you in a budget cycle. So the incentive structure inside city government quietly selects for consultants over operators, every time.

This is not unique to struggling cities. It shows up in Baltimore, Phoenix, and Detroit alike. The sophistication of the consulting deck scales with the size of the city's budget. The operational gap does not close.

What Detroit Reveals About the Pattern

Detroit is a useful case study because the stakes were never abstract. After the 2013 bankruptcy — the largest municipal bankruptcy in U.S. history at roughly $18 billion in debt — the city became a laboratory. Consultants flooded in. McKinsey, KPMG, Ernst and Young, and dozens of smaller firms all had Detroit engagements. Many of them produced genuinely useful diagnostics. The city's financial controls improved. Blight data got mapped. Processes got documented.

But the persistent gaps — workforce pipeline into manufacturing, small business access to capital in neighborhoods, affordable housing unit production — those did not close through consulting. They began to move, where they moved at all, when operators entered the picture. When people who had actually built workforce programs in other cities, or had underwritten community development loans, or had managed a housing portfolio at scale got into rooms with decision authority. The difference is not the quality of the analysis. It is whether the person giving the advice has ever had to live with the consequences of being wrong.

Detroit also shows the cost of the lag. A consulting engagement typically runs six to eighteen months before any recommendation reaches implementation. An operator embedded in a system can identify the same problem in thirty days and start testing a fix. At the scale of a city, that lag is not administrative friction. It is years of compounded inaction.

The difference is not the quality of the analysis. It is whether the person giving the advice has ever had to live with the consequences of being wrong.

The Credential Problem

Part of why cities default to consultants is that consulting firms have built a credentialing infrastructure that municipal procurement recognizes. ISO certifications, past government contract lists, liability insurance structures, Dun and Bradstreet numbers — all of it creates a legible, low-risk vendor profile. Operators, particularly smaller operators or those coming from nonprofit or entrepreneurial backgrounds, often cannot check those boxes fast enough to compete.

A 2022 analysis by the National League of Cities found that fewer than 25 percent of cities had formal mechanisms to evaluate vendor track records based on operational outcomes rather than process compliance. That means the majority of municipal procurement is selecting for vendors who are good at winning contracts, not vendors who are good at running systems. Those are related skills, but they are not the same skill.

Chicago has experimented with outcome-based contracting in some of its workforce development programs, tying a portion of vendor payment to job placement and 90-day retention rates. The results were mixed, but the direction was right. You cannot fix the consultant-operator gap purely through contract design, but you can stop making it structurally impossible for operators to win.

What Operators Actually Bring

An operator brings a different kind of knowledge than a consultant. Not better, necessarily — but different in ways that matter for implementation. A consultant brings pattern recognition across many cities and sectors. An operator brings scar tissue from having watched a specific type of plan fail at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday when the system did not behave the way the model said it would.

That scar tissue shows up in how operators write scope. They build in contingencies that consultants skip because consultants will not be there when the contingency triggers. Operators know which metrics look good in a report and which metrics actually tell you whether the program is working. They know the difference between a community meeting that generates buy-in and one that generates the appearance of buy-in. These are not insights you get from research. They come from running things.

The cities that have made genuine structural progress — not just analytical progress — tend to be the ones that found ways to put operators inside the decision loop, not just consultants in the advisory role. That does not mean cutting consultants out. It means being honest about what each brings and building procurement and governance structures that can hold both.

The Fix Is Not Simple, But It Is Visible

Fixing this requires changes at three levels. Procurement has to evolve so that operational track records can be evaluated and weighted alongside process compliance. City leadership has to get comfortable with accountability concentration — with hiring people who can be held responsible when a system does not perform. And operators, frankly, have to get better at translating their experience into the language that municipal procurement understands, without losing the substance of what makes them valuable.

None of these changes are technically complex. They require political will and a willingness to tolerate a different kind of risk — performance risk instead of process risk. That is a harder sell inside government than it sounds, because process risk is familiar and performance risk feels personal. But we are long past the point where familiar risk management is producing the outcomes cities need.

Detroit, in particular, does not have the luxury of another decade of frameworks and recommendations. Neither do the communities in cities like Baltimore or Cleveland that have been studied, surveyed, and reported on for thirty years without the structural conditions changing. The analysis has been done. What is missing is the operator in the room with the authority to run the fix.

The consultant-operator gap is not a purchasing problem. It is a governance problem dressed up as a purchasing problem. Cities keep buying recommendations because recommendations are safe, and running systems is not. Until the people who fund and govern cities decide that performance accountability is worth the discomfort it creates, the pattern will hold. The next question worth asking is what it would actually take to structure a city engagement so that an operator could win it — and what an operator-first procurement framework would need to look like to survive political cycles. That is where the real design work starts.
