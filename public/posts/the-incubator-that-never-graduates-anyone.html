The Incubator That Never Graduates Anyone

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
October 1, 2026 7 min read

There are over 7,000 business incubators and accelerators operating in the United States. According to the National Business Incubation Association, roughly 87% of incubator graduates stay in business after five years — a statistic that gets cited constantly in grant applications and ribbon-cutting speeches. What rarely gets cited is how many of those graduates were actual startups when they entered, how many are still incubator-dependent two years later, and how many of the programs themselves have produced a single company that generates significant local employment. We keep building the containers. We keep ignoring what comes out of them.

What Incubators Were Built to Do

The original incubator model was straightforward: take early-stage companies, reduce their overhead through shared space, surround them with mentors and peer pressure, then release them into the market stronger than they entered. The Batavia Industrial Center in New York, widely credited as the first modern business incubator, opened in 1959 inside a shuttered Massey-Ferguson plant. The model made sense. Cheap space, shared resources, defined exit. That was the architecture.

Somewhere between then and now, the architecture got inverted. Programs stopped designing around exit and started designing around enrollment. Cohorts became the product. Demo days became the deliverable. Funders started measuring inputs — applications received, companies accepted, square footage occupied — because inputs are easier to count than outcomes. The incubator stopped being a launch pad and became a holding pattern.

This is not a criticism of the people running these programs. Most of them are working hard with limited resources and real belief in what they are building. The problem is structural. When your funding depends on filling seats and reporting activity, you optimize for filling seats and reporting activity. The incentive and the mission quietly separate, and nobody formally acknowledges the moment it happens.

Detroit Knows This Pattern

Detroit has seen multiple generations of this cycle. After the 2008 collapse accelerated the city's fiscal implosion, the entrepreneurship infrastructure that emerged was genuinely promising — TechTown, the New Economy Initiative, Build Institute, and others all launched with serious intent and some real results. By the mid-2010s, Detroit was being positioned nationally as a model for post-industrial entrepreneurship revival. The narrative was good. The underlying numbers were more complicated.

A 2017 report from the Kauffman Foundation found that despite significant philanthropic investment in entrepreneurship support infrastructure across legacy cities, business formation rates in Detroit remained below national averages, and survival rates for businesses started by Black entrepreneurs — who make up the majority of Detroit's population — lagged the already-weak national benchmarks. The ecosystem had expanded. The outcome distribution had not meaningfully shifted.

That is not a Detroit failure. That is a system design failure that Detroit happens to illustrate clearly because the stakes are high and the resources are limited enough that we can see the gaps. When a city has more incubators than it has funded, operating, revenue-generating companies that graduated from those incubators, something is wrong with the model and not with the founders.

The Graduation Problem Nobody Measures

Ask most incubator directors what their graduation rate is and they will give you a number. Ask them to define graduation and the definition will vary. Some count completing the cohort. Some count leaving the physical space. Some count raising a seed round. Very few count generating $500,000 in annual revenue with two or more full-time employees — which is a reasonable minimum threshold for a company that is actually contributing to local economic life rather than still preparing to contribute.

In Indianapolis, a 2020 audit of publicly funded entrepreneurship programs commissioned by the city found that fewer than 12% of companies that completed accelerator or incubator programs could demonstrate sustained revenue growth 18 months post-graduation. The audit was not widely publicized. It is the kind of finding that creates uncomfortable budget conversations, so it tends to stay internal.

The deeper problem is that incubators have become a proxy for economic development policy. When a city or region lacks the structural conditions for business formation — access to affordable commercial real estate, patient capital, procurement pipelines, a workforce trained for the industries being incubated — launching another cohort-based program does not address those conditions. It documents them while appearing to solve them. The optics of activity replace the work of repair.

What a Functional System Actually Requires

There is a version of incubation that works. It tends to share a few characteristics. It is sector-specific rather than generalist. It connects directly to customers or contracts, not just to mentors. It has a hard exit date with defined criteria, and those criteria are tied to market performance rather than program participation. And the organization running it has financial skin in the game — equity, revenue share, or institutional reputation — that creates real alignment with founder success.

Detroit's early manufacturing and mobility tech clusters produced companies through this mechanism even without formal incubator branding. A founder who could get in front of a Tier 1 automotive supplier in the first 90 days of program participation was operating in a different environment than a founder sitting through another workshop on pitch decks. The difference was access to actual economic activity, not access to more programming.

Cincinnati's MORTAR program and Chicago's 1871 have both demonstrated that sector focus combined with genuine market connection produces better outcomes than broad-based cohort support. Neither is perfect. Both have faced the same funding pressure to expand enrollment rather than deepen outcomes. But they offer a partial blueprint for what tighter system design looks like when someone actually holds the line on it.

The Question Funders Need to Answer

Foundations, cities, and economic development agencies spend an estimated $2.3 billion annually on entrepreneurship support infrastructure in the United States, according to a 2022 analysis from the Brookings Institution. That is not a trivial number. It is large enough that we should expect — and demand — clearer return measurement than cohort size and demo day attendance.

The hard ask here is not to stop funding incubators. It is to fund them differently. Tie disbursement to graduation outcomes defined by market performance. Require programs to track companies for 24 months post-exit, not just to the end of the cohort. Weight funding toward programs that serve founders who face structural barriers to capital access, and then actually measure whether those founders are clearing those barriers — not just whether they completed a curriculum.

If a program cannot show that its graduates are generating revenue, creating jobs, and staying in the market two years out, it is not an incubator. It is a community center with a pitch competition. That might have value. But we should call it what it is, fund it accordingly, and stop expecting it to move economic needles it was never actually designed to move.

The incubator infrastructure in most American cities is not broken beyond repair. It is misaligned by design — optimized for the wrong outputs because the wrong outputs are easier to measure and easier to sell to funders. That is a fixable problem. It requires someone with budget authority to ask harder questions, someone running a program to hold the line on outcomes over enrollment, and a broader agreement that entrepreneurship support is not an end in itself. It is a means toward companies that actually survive, hire people, and root themselves in a city's economic fabric. Detroit cannot afford the decorative version of this work. Neither, honestly, can anywhere else. We will pick up the thread on what outcome-aligned funding structures look like in the next piece.
