What Real Public-Private Partnerships Look Like

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
May 28, 2026 4 min read

The Federal Highway Administration reports that 78% of public-private partnerships in infrastructure fail to meet their original performance targets. This statistic reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of what these partnerships should accomplish. We've been measuring the wrong outcomes while missing the actual value creation happening beneath the surface.

The Measurement Problem

Most public-private partnerships get evaluated on cost savings and timeline adherence. These metrics miss the point entirely. The real value emerges in capability transfer, risk distribution, and long-term operational efficiency. When we focus on upfront costs, we optimize for the wrong variables.

Detroit's M-1 Rail project offers a clear example. Critics pointed to cost overruns and construction delays as evidence of failure. But the partnership created something more valuable: a template for transit development that other cities could replicate. The knowledge transfer between Quicken Loans, Kresge Foundation, and the federal government established operational frameworks that extend far beyond one streetcar line.

The partnership also demonstrated how private capital could de-risk public infrastructure investment. Private partners absorbed market uncertainty while public entities provided regulatory stability. This risk distribution model now influences how Detroit approaches larger transit projects.

Operational Integration

Successful partnerships integrate operations from day one rather than treating collaboration as an add-on. Denver's Union Station redevelopment illustrates this principle. The Regional Transportation District didn't just hire private developers to build a train station. They created a unified operating entity that manages transit, retail, and real estate as one ecosystem.

This integration required new governance structures. Public transit authority, private real estate developers, and retail operators share decision-making responsibility through a joint management board. Revenue from commercial activities subsidizes transit operations. Property values increase because transit access improves. Each component strengthens the others.

The key insight: partnerships work when all parties have aligned financial incentives over time. One-time contracts create adversarial relationships. Ongoing revenue sharing creates collaborative ones.

Effective partnerships create scalable models rather than one-off projects—the partnership structure itself becomes a product that other cities can implement.

Knowledge Transfer Systems

The most valuable partnerships create permanent knowledge transfer between sectors. Private companies learn how public accountability works. Public agencies learn how private efficiency operates. This bilateral learning compounds over multiple projects.

Detroit's Strategic Neighborhood Fund demonstrates this dynamic. JPMorgan Chase didn't just provide $125 million in funding. They embedded their project management systems within Detroit's planning department. City staff learned private sector due diligence processes. Bank analysts learned community engagement protocols.

Five years later, Detroit's planning capacity has fundamentally improved. Staff can evaluate development proposals with sophisticated financial modeling. They understand market dynamics that were previously opaque. This capability persists regardless of future partnerships. The knowledge transfer created lasting institutional improvement.

Scale and Replication

Effective partnerships create scalable models rather than one-off projects. The partnership structure itself becomes a product that other cities can implement. This requires designing partnerships for replication from the beginning.

Consider how Detroit's land bank partnership with private developers created standardized acquisition and disposition processes. These processes now operate across multiple neighborhoods with different private partners. The system scales because the partnership established clear protocols, not just individual deals.

Cincinnati adapted Detroit's land bank model for their own context. They didn't copy the specific partnerships but implemented the systemic approach to public-private coordination. This is how successful partnerships compound: through institutional learning that travels between cities.

Performance Metrics That Matter

We need different success metrics. Instead of measuring cost savings, measure capability building. Instead of timeline adherence, measure knowledge transfer. Instead of single-project outcomes, measure systemic improvement.

Real performance indicators include: How many public employees gained new skills? How many private partners understand public accountability requirements? How many similar partnerships became easier to execute? How much did overall city capacity increase?

According to the Brookings Institution, cities with five or more successful public-private partnerships show 23% faster economic development project completion times compared to cities with traditional procurement approaches. This metric captures the true value: improved municipal capacity that accelerates future growth.

Public-private partnerships succeed when they build systems instead of completing transactions. The goal isn't cheaper government or higher private profits. The goal is creating institutional capacity that makes cities more effective at solving complex problems. When we measure partnerships against this standard, we start designing them differently. And when we design them differently, they start working.
