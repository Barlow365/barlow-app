Why the Gig Economy Is a Systems Failure Dressed as Flexibility

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
July 25, 2026 7 min read

About 59 million Americans performed gig work in 2023, according to Statista — roughly 36% of the U.S. workforce. That number gets cited as proof of a thriving flexible economy. What it actually measures is how many people we've moved off stable employment and onto a structure with no floor. When a third of the workforce operates without employer-provided benefits, predictable income, or legal protections that workers spent a century earning, that is not a feature. That is a failure mode.

The Architecture of the Problem

Every broken system looks functional from the outside until you examine what it's actually optimizing for. The gig economy optimizes for platform revenue and labor cost reduction. It does that well. What it does not do is build stable households, fund retirements, or create the kind of consistent consumer spending that holds a local economy together.

The classification of workers as independent contractors is the load-bearing wall here. The moment a worker is classified as a contractor, the employer obligation disappears — no payroll tax contribution, no workers' compensation, no unemployment insurance, no overtime protection. The worker absorbs all of that risk personally. The platform captures the upside of scale. That asymmetry is not a market outcome. It is an engineered one.

We have seen this pattern before. Detroit in the 1970s and 80s watched auto manufacturers offshore production and fragment their supply chains, pushing risk down to smaller suppliers and eventually to workers. The gig economy runs the same play, just faster and with a better app. The mechanism is identical: disaggregate the workforce until no single employer is responsible for any of it.

What the Numbers Actually Show

The Economic Policy Institute found that gig workers earn about 58 cents for every dollar earned by comparable traditionally employed workers when you account for hours worked and benefit value. That gap does not reflect different skill levels. It reflects a structural tax on workers who lack the bargaining position to demand anything different.

In cities like Chicago and Detroit, where manufacturing decline already hollowed out the middle-wage labor market, gig work did not fill the gap — it papered over it. A delivery driver in Detroit making $18 an hour before expenses sounds like reasonable wages until you subtract vehicle depreciation, fuel, insurance, and self-employment taxes. The MIT Sloan Management Review put median hourly earnings for Uber drivers after expenses at roughly $8.55 per hour in an earlier study. The flexibility is real. The compensation is not.

There is also the retirement problem, and it compounds quietly. Traditional employment triggers automatic payroll deductions and, often, employer matching. Gig work does neither. A worker spending five to ten years in the gig economy during their prime earning years is not just earning less — they are building nothing for later. That cost does not show up in the platform's quarterly earnings report. It shows up in a generation of people reaching retirement age with nothing.

Flexibility and exploitation are not the same thing, and we have been letting them share a zip code for too long.

The Flexibility Argument Does Not Hold the Weight We Put on It

The defense of the gig model usually starts and ends with flexibility. Workers choose their hours. They work when they want. That is worth something, and we should not dismiss it entirely. Some workers — caregivers, students, people managing chronic illness — genuinely benefit from non-standard scheduling. The problem is that flexibility has been used to justify an entire compensation and protection structure that serves the platform, not the worker.

When surveys ask gig workers why they do it, a significant portion report that they do gig work because they cannot find traditional employment, not because flexibility is their priority. The Federal Reserve's Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households has consistently shown that a meaningful share of gig workers are doing it out of necessity. Calling that a choice is the kind of framing that lets a systems failure get rebranded as consumer preference.

In Los Angeles, where Proposition 22 passed in 2020 after platforms spent over $200 million to classify their workers as contractors, the aftermath has been studied closely. Workers got a wage floor and some limited benefits — but far below what full employee status would have provided. The proposition became a template other states have considered. That is the system defending itself, not reforming itself.

Where the System Actually Needs Repair

Diagnosing a failure is only useful if it points toward a fix. There are three structural levers worth examining seriously. First, portable benefits — the idea that benefit entitlements attach to the worker rather than the employer. Senator Mark Warner has proposed versions of this for years. It would allow a worker to accumulate health care contributions, retirement contributions, and paid leave credits regardless of who they work for in a given week. It does not require reclassifying anyone. It repairs the floor.

Second, algorithmic accountability. Right now, platforms set pay rates, deactivate workers, and allocate jobs through systems that workers cannot audit or appeal in any meaningful way. That is not independent contracting — that is employment without the protections. Some version of transparency and appeal rights for algorithmic management is a reasonable regulatory expectation, and several EU member states have already moved in that direction.

Third, we need to stop measuring labor market health by headline employment numbers alone. Detroit's workforce development organizations have been doing the harder work of asking what jobs actually pay and what they build toward. That lens — quality, not just quantity — is the right diagnostic frame. A city that replaces 10,000 manufacturing jobs with 10,000 gig positions has not recovered. It has reclassified its problem.

The Bigger Picture We Keep Avoiding

The gig economy is not an aberration. It is the logical endpoint of three decades of decisions about who bears risk in the labor market. Every time we made it easier to classify workers as contractors, every time we defunded labor enforcement, every time we let the benefits cliff between employment types widen — we built the conditions for this.

The platforms did not create the vulnerability. They found it and scaled it. That distinction matters because it means the fix is not about regulating one industry. It is about rebuilding the baseline infrastructure that makes work worth doing — portable benefits, enforceable standards, and a definition of employment that reflects how work actually happens now, not how it happened in 1938 when the Fair Labor Standards Act was written.

We are not going to legislate gig work out of existence, nor should we try. The scheduling flexibility has genuine value for some workers and real operational utility for some industries. But flexibility and exploitation are not the same thing, and we have been letting them share a zip code for too long. The next question is what a portable, platform-neutral labor floor actually looks like — and who has the political will to build it.

The gig economy is a stress test that the existing labor system failed. That is the honest read. The workers absorbing that failure — in Detroit, in Los Angeles, in every mid-size city where traditional employment dried up — are not experiencing disruption. They are experiencing the downstream cost of decisions made long before any app existed. Getting this right means rebuilding infrastructure, not just regulating platforms. That work is slower, less visible, and more important than anything the platforms are building. We will get into what that infrastructure actually looks like in the next piece.
