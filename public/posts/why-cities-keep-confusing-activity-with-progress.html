Why Cities Keep Confusing Activity With Progress

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
October 17, 2026 7 min read

Between 2010 and 2020, Detroit received billions in public and private investment. New stadiums, a QLine streetcar, renovated downtown corridors, and a flood of press coverage about the city's comeback. Over that same decade, Detroit's poverty rate held stubbornly above 30 percent — more than triple the national average. The cranes were real. The progress was selective. That gap between visible activity and measurable improvement is not unique to Detroit. It is a systemic failure that most cities are still unwilling to name directly.

What Activity Actually Signals

Activity is easy to photograph. A groundbreaking ceremony, a new mixed-use development, a task force launch — these are legible signals that leadership is doing something. And that is precisely the problem. They are optimized for the appearance of motion, not the mechanics of change.

City administrators and developers are rational actors. They respond to the incentives in front of them. When media coverage, grant cycles, and political calendars all reward announcements over outcomes, the system produces announcements. Nobody cuts a ribbon on a poverty rate that dropped three points. Nobody holds a press conference because a workforce pipeline quietly placed 400 people into stable jobs over 18 months. The work that actually moves systems rarely produces a good photo.

This is not cynicism about intent. Most of the people driving these investments believe in what they are building. The diagnosis is about structure, not character. When the measurement framework is broken, even well-intentioned effort gets misdirected.

The Metrics Problem Underneath Everything

Cities tend to track inputs and outputs, not outcomes. Inputs are dollars spent. Outputs are units built, events held, businesses opened. Outcomes are what actually changed in people's lives — income stability, housing cost burden, educational attainment, health access. Most municipal reporting stops at the output layer and calls it success.

Baltimore offers a useful case study in this distinction. The city invested heavily in its Inner Harbor redevelopment over several decades, producing a tourism district that draws millions of visitors annually. At the same time, Baltimore consistently ranks among the most violent cities in the country, with a homicide rate that reached 56 per 100,000 residents in 2017, according to FBI crime data. The harbor thrived. Entire neighborhoods remained structurally abandoned. Both things were true simultaneously, and the activity in one place was frequently cited as evidence that the city was turning a corner.

The error is treating concentrated success as distributed progress. When one zip code gentrifies while three adjacent ones hollow out further, the aggregate numbers can look deceptively stable. Averages obscure geography. And geography is where people actually live.

A fragmented ecosystem of activity is not the same as a functioning system — and the difference between those two things determines whether people actually get placed into jobs that pay enough to stay in the city.

How the Announcement Cycle Distorts Planning

Most city planning operates on political timelines, not systems timelines. An elected official has a four-year window. Real systems change — in workforce development, in housing affordability, in neighborhood stabilization — operates on ten to twenty year cycles. That mismatch creates structural pressure to compress real work into announcement-sized chunks that can be claimed within a term.

The result is a proliferation of initiatives. Studies commission more studies. Pilot programs launch without paths to scale. Task forces produce reports that inform the next task force. In Detroit, the number of workforce development organizations operating in the city at any given moment has historically been difficult to count precisely because they are not well coordinated — a dynamic that a 2018 report from the Workforce Intelligence Network documented across Southeast Michigan, noting fragmentation as a primary barrier to workforce system effectiveness.

Each individual organization doing workforce training is doing something real. But a fragmented ecosystem of activity is not the same as a functioning system. The difference between those two things determines whether people actually get placed into jobs that pay enough to stay in the city.

Phoenix and the Infrastructure Illusion

Phoenix built more light rail miles per capita than almost any comparable Sun Belt city over the past two decades. The infrastructure is real and functional. But Phoenix also has one of the highest rates of car-dependency in the country, and transit ridership has remained a fraction of what projections anticipated. The investment happened. The behavioral and land-use conditions that would have made the investment perform as intended did not follow.

This is the infrastructure illusion: the assumption that building the physical layer is sufficient to produce the systemic outcome. Transit works when it is embedded in density, walkability, and land-use patterns that make it the rational choice for enough people. Drop a train line into a sprawling, car-oriented grid and you get a train line — not a transit system. The distinction matters enormously for how we evaluate whether a city made progress or made an announcement.

Detroit faces a similar dynamic with broadband infrastructure investments and with its own transit expansions. The asset exists. Whether the asset is integrated into a functioning system that serves residents who need it most is a separate and harder question.

What Progress Actually Requires

Progress requires feedback loops that are honest, slow, and boring. It requires tracking whether the people who were supposed to benefit actually benefited — not whether the program launched on time and under budget. It requires the willingness to shut down initiatives that are producing outputs but not outcomes, which is politically difficult because those initiatives have constituencies and press releases attached to them.

It also requires accepting that some of the most important work is invisible at the systems level until it has been running long enough to accumulate. Stabilizing a block of housing, keeping a small employer from leaving a neighborhood, running a consistent after-school program for five straight years — none of these produce announcement-worthy moments. All of them contribute to the conditions in which larger change becomes possible.

The cities that have actually shifted trajectory over time share one common trait: they built measurement infrastructure alongside physical infrastructure. They tracked who was moving into stability, not just what was being built. They treated resident outcomes as the primary variable, not the secondary one. That reorientation is harder than a groundbreaking. It does not photograph well. But it is the actual work.

The confusion between activity and progress is not inevitable. It is a choice embedded in how cities measure, report, and reward their own work. Fixing it starts with asking a different first question — not what did we build, but who is measurably better off and why. That question is uncomfortable because it demands accountability across longer timelines than most political structures are designed to tolerate. But it is the only question that leads somewhere other than the next ribbon cutting. What that accountability infrastructure actually looks like, and which cities are beginning to build it, is worth examining next.
