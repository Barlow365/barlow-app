Why Most Innovation Labs Don't Innovate

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
July 11, 2026 7 min read

According to a 2019 Capgemini study, 90% of executives said innovation was a strategic priority, yet fewer than 20% reported being satisfied with their innovation outcomes. That gap is not a motivation problem. It is a structural one. Most organizations build innovation labs the way cities build convention centers — as a signal, not a system. The building goes up, the ribbon gets cut, and then everyone waits for something to happen.

The Lab as Trophy Asset

Walk into most corporate or civic innovation labs and you will find the same inventory: standing desks, whiteboards covered in sticky notes, a ping-pong table that nobody uses past the first month, and a carefully curated selection of books about disruption. What you will not find is a clear line between what happens inside that room and any decision that affects the core operation.

That is the tell. When a lab is physically and organizationally separated from where the real work happens, it is not a laboratory. It is a showroom. It exists to demonstrate that the organization takes the future seriously, not to actually engage with it. Detroit went through a version of this with several automotive supplier innovation centers in the mid-2010s — impressive facilities that generated patents and press releases while the actual production lines kept running on the same assumptions they had used for decades.

The trophy asset problem is not unique to corporations. Cities do it too. Pittsburgh built innovation district branding around its Oakland neighborhood while struggling to connect the university research happening there to the workforce development needs of residents on the other side of the Monongahela. The geography of innovation theater is remarkably consistent across sectors.

Separated from Consequence

The deeper structural flaw is consequence separation. When the people running an innovation lab do not bear the cost of being wrong, they optimize for the wrong things. They optimize for interesting, for novel, for fundable. They do not optimize for solving the actual problem in front of the organization.

A team embedded in operations — a logistics manager trying to cut fulfillment errors, a school principal trying to reduce chronic absenteeism — innovates constantly. They have no choice. The feedback loop is immediate and the stakes are real. Put that same energy in a lab with a two-year runway and a mandate to 'think big,' and the urgency disappears. What replaces it is a culture of pilots that never scale and reports that never get implemented.

McKinsey research has found that roughly 70% of corporate transformation efforts fail to achieve their goals. A significant portion of that failure traces back to the disconnect between the teams tasked with change and the operational reality they are supposed to be changing. Innovation labs are often the most visible symptom of that disconnect.

A successful pilot that cannot scale is not innovation — it is a science fair project.

The Staffing Trap

Most organizations staff innovation labs with one of two profiles: high-potential internal candidates who get rotated in as a career development opportunity, or external hires brought in specifically because they are 'not like us.' Both approaches create problems.

The internal rotational model produces people who know they are leaving in eighteen months. They are not going to push hard against the organizational immune system because they still need to work with those people on the other side of the rotation. The external hire model produces people who understand trends but not context. They arrive with frameworks and leave before those frameworks ever get stress-tested against the real constraints of the organization.

What actually works is a different staffing logic: people who have deep operational credibility inside the organization and genuine permission to challenge the way things work. Not a separate class of innovators. Operators with mandate. Detroit's most effective manufacturing turnarounds in the post-2009 period were led by engineers who had been on the floor for fifteen years, not by consultants parachuted in from outside. The pattern holds.

The Metrics Tell the Story

Ask a lab director how they measure success and you will learn everything about whether the lab is real. If the answer involves patents filed, ideas generated, workshops facilitated, or press mentions, the lab is measuring activity. If the answer involves revenue generated, cost reduced, processes changed, or problems closed, you might be talking to someone running a real operation.

Activity metrics are seductive because they are easy to hit. A team can run fifty design sprints a year and call that productivity. But design sprints that do not connect to budget authority, procurement decisions, or operational change are elaborate exercises. They produce artifacts, not outcomes.

Chicago's city government experimented with civic innovation offices throughout the 2010s with mixed results. The offices that survived and produced real change were the ones that embedded directly with department commissioners who had operational control and accountability. The ones that ran independently as 'innovation teams' accumulated good ideas that sat in Google Drives. The distinction was not talent. It was structural connection to consequence.

What a Real Innovation Function Looks Like

The fix is not complicated, but it requires accepting that most of what organizations call innovation infrastructure is actually communications infrastructure. It signals commitment rather than producing it. Once you accept that, you can build something that actually functions.

Real innovation capacity lives close to the problem. It has a short feedback loop between hypothesis and evidence. It has budget authority over its own pilots, even modest ones. It reports to someone who has operational accountability, not just strategic vision. And critically, it has a defined process for escalating a working idea into the core operation — because a successful pilot that cannot scale is not innovation, it is a science fair project.

We are not arguing that dedicated teams are always wrong. There are legitimate reasons to protect exploratory work from quarterly pressure. But protection is different from isolation. The goal is a membrane that filters short-term noise while keeping the team connected to real organizational metabolism. Most labs build walls instead of membranes, and then wonder why nothing gets out.

Detroit is in the middle of an interesting test case right now. Several of the organizations rebuilding workforce pipelines for the EV transition are doing it through embedded operational teams rather than standalone innovation offices. The early evidence is that proximity to the actual hiring constraint — the specific gap between available workers and the skills employers need — is producing faster iteration than any lab format would allow. That is not a coincidence. It is a systems property.

The innovation lab problem is ultimately a power problem. Labs without authority cannot change systems. They can document them, map them, prototype around them, and occasionally embarrass them. But structural change requires structural access. The organizations getting real traction right now — in manufacturing, in civic services, in workforce development — are not the ones with the best-designed lab spaces. They are the ones that figured out how to give people with good ideas a direct line to the levers that matter. That is where we should be putting our attention next.
