The Org Chart Is Not the Organization

Jonathan Barlow Life Engineer
September 19, 2026 7 min read

A 2023 McKinsey study found that 70% of large-scale organizational transformations fail to meet their objectives. Leadership teams look at that number and blame culture, market conditions, or timing. The more honest diagnosis is simpler: they were managing the org chart instead of the organization. Those are two different things, and confusing them is one of the most expensive mistakes a builder can make.

What the Chart Actually Shows

The org chart shows authority. It shows who reports to whom, who signs the budget requests, who holds the title. That information is real and it matters — but it maps a skeleton, not a living system. It tells you nothing about who people actually call when a deal is about to fall apart at 4pm on a Friday. It tells you nothing about which mid-level manager has spent twelve years building the informal trust that keeps two departments from open warfare.

We built a manufacturing operation in Detroit where the org chart said one person owned quality control. Technically accurate. But in practice, a line supervisor three levels down had quietly become the institutional memory for defect patterns going back eight years. When we reorganized and moved her to a different floor, defect rates climbed within six weeks. The chart never captured her. The organization absolutely depended on her.

This is not a rare edge case. This is how every organization above a certain size actually functions. The formal structure and the operational structure drift apart the moment the ink dries on the original design.

The Cost of Managing the Wrong Map

When leaders manage to the chart, they make decisions based on incomplete information dressed up as complete information. They restructure reporting lines thinking they are redirecting power, when really they are just moving boxes around while the actual network of influence and knowledge remains exactly where it was — or gets quietly disrupted in ways that won't show up in a quarterly review.

Gallup's 2023 State of the Global Workplace report puts global employee engagement at 23%. That number is not just about morale. It is a signal that most workers have figured out that the official system and the real system do not align, and they have stopped investing effort in bridging the gap. When people feel that who you know and which informal network you belong to matters more than the structure you are supposed to operate inside, they disengage from the structure entirely.

In Detroit, we have watched entire municipal departments stall on execution because politically appointed directors could not locate the actual decision-making nerve centers inside their own organizations. The chart said they had authority. The institution told a different story. The same pattern played out in Baltimore's public school system during its 2016 administrative overhaul, where restructuring authority on paper did almost nothing to change outcomes for years because the informal networks of principals and curriculum leads operated on a completely separate logic.

The formal structure and the operational structure drift apart the moment the ink dries on the original design.

What the Real Organization Looks Like

The real organization is a network of relationships, habits, information flows, and informal agreements that have accumulated over time. Some of it is intentional. Most of it is not. It includes the standing meeting that everybody knows is where real approvals happen even though it has no formal authority. It includes the two department heads who went through a bad product launch together six years ago and now have a trust level that no reporting structure could replicate. It includes the analyst who gets cc'd on everything because she reliably knows where the bodies are buried.

Organizational network analysis — a methodology that maps actual communication and collaboration patterns rather than formal reporting — has started to make this visible. Companies using it have found that in organizations of 150 people, a handful of critical connectors often sit nowhere near the top of the chart. Harvard Business Review research has shown that in many firms, 3 to 5 percent of employees generate 20 to 35 percent of the value-added collaboration. Those people are rarely the ones with the biggest titles.

In Pittsburgh, when Highmark Health undertook a major operational consolidation, the leaders who made the most progress were the ones who spent time mapping those informal connectors before they moved anything structural. The ones who started with the org chart and worked outward struggled significantly longer.

What Builders Do Differently

A builder diagnosing an organization starts by asking: who do people actually call? Not who are they supposed to call. Who do they call. That one question, asked enough times across enough levels, draws a map that looks almost nothing like the official version — and it is infinitely more useful.

Before a reorganization, before a new hire at the senior level, before a process change that touches multiple departments, we try to locate the load-bearing relationships. Which informal agreements are currently holding things together that nobody has documented? Which connectors, if moved or removed, would create gaps that the formal structure has no mechanism to fill? This is not sentimental. It is structural. You cannot build on a foundation you refuse to look at clearly.

In Detroit, rebuilding institutional capacity in distressed neighborhoods has taught us this lesson repeatedly. The block club president who does not show up on any organizational chart is often the single most important person in a community's ability to execute anything. Fund the nonprofit above her, redesign the program, restructure the grant requirements — none of it matters if you have not figured out where she fits and what she needs to stay engaged. The org chart for that neighborhood looks nothing like what actually governs behavior on the ground.

The Fix Is Not Another Reorganization

The instinct when things are not working is to redraw the chart. New boxes, new lines, new titles. It feels like action. It creates the optics of decisive leadership. And it often makes things measurably worse in the short term while solving almost none of the underlying problems, because the underlying problem is almost never the chart itself.

The fix is to spend real time understanding the organization you actually have before you redesign the organization you wish you had. Map the informal networks. Find the connectors. Identify the load-bearing relationships and the unofficial decision points. Build your formal structure in ways that support and reinforce those patterns rather than cutting across them arbitrarily.

That work is slower, less visible, and harder to explain to a board. It also has a significantly better track record than the alternative. The leaders who consistently execute are not the ones who draw the cleanest org charts. They are the ones who understand that the chart is a starting point for a conversation, not a description of reality.

Every system has a visible architecture and a working architecture. The gap between them is not a sign that something has gone wrong — it is a sign that the system is alive and has been adapting. Our job as builders is to close that gap deliberately, not pretend it does not exist. The next question worth sitting with: if your org chart disappeared tomorrow, would your organization still know how to function? If the answer is yes, you have built something real. If the answer is unclear, you have more mapping to do before you touch anything else.
